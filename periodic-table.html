How to read the periodic table

The periodic table organises every known element by its atomic structure. Learn what its rows, columns and colours tell you, and browse data for the first 36 elements.

the layout

Rows, columns and blocks

Periods are the horizontal rows. Moving left to right, each element has one more proton than the last. Elements in the same period have the same number of occupied electron shells.

Groups are the vertical columns, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have the same number of outer electrons, so they often behave in similar ways. The alkali metals in group 1, for example, all react readily with water.

The table is also divided into metals on the left and centre, nonmetals on the upper right, and metalloids along the staircase between them.

trends

Patterns across the table

Atomic radius

Atoms get smaller across a period, as more protons pull electrons closer, and larger down a group, as shells are added.

Electronegativity

Increases across a period and decreases down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

Metallic character

Decreases across a period and increases down a group. The most reactive metals sit at the bottom left.

history

A table that predicted the future

In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements by atomic mass and noticed that their properties repeated in a regular pattern. Boldly, he left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered and predicted their properties.

When gallium and germanium were found in the following years, their properties closely matched his predictions. Today’s table is ordered by atomic number rather than mass, but it still reflects Mendeleev’s central insight.
