Chemistry study guides

Clear, concise explanations of the core topics in introductory chemistry, each with a worked example you can check yourself.

how to use these guides

Read, try, explain

Each guide covers one big idea. Read it once for the overall picture, then work through the example without looking. Finally, try explaining the idea in your own words or teaching it to someone else.

The guides follow the same order as our learning roadmap, so beginners can start at the top and work down.

guide 01

Atomic structure

Every atom has a tiny, dense nucleus containing protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral), surrounded by electrons (negative) arranged in shells. The number of protons is the atomic number; protons plus neutrons give the mass number.

Example: a carbon-12 atom has 6 protons, 6 neutrons and 6 electrons, arranged 2 in the first shell and 4 in the second.
guide 02

Chemical bonding

Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of outer electrons. In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred from a metal to a nonmetal, forming oppositely charged ions. In covalent bonding, nonmetal atoms share pairs of electrons. In metallic bonding, metal ions sit in a sea of shared electrons.

Example: sodium gives one electron to chlorine, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that attract to make sodium chloride.
guide 03

The mole and molar mass

A mole is 6.022 × 10²³ particles, known as Avogadro’s constant. The molar mass of a substance, in g/mol, equals the sum of the atomic masses in its formula. Use moles = mass ÷ molar mass.

Example: 36 g of water ÷ 18.02 g/mol = 2.0 moles of water molecules.
guide 04

Writing and balancing equations

A chemical equation shows reactants turning into products. To balance it, change only the coefficients in front of formulas, never the subscripts inside them, until each element has the same number of atoms on both sides.

Example: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O becomes 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, with four hydrogen and two oxygen atoms on each side.
guide 05

Types of reactions

Common categories include synthesis (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B), displacement (A + BC → AC + B) and combustion, where a fuel reacts with oxygen to release energy.

Example: when magnesium burns in air, it combines with oxygen in a synthesis reaction to form magnesium oxide.
guide 06

Acids, bases and pH

Acids release hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water; bases accept them or release hydroxide ions (OH⁻). The pH scale runs from 0 to 14: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is alkaline. When an acid and a base react, they neutralise each other to form a salt and water.

Example: lemon juice is acidic (around pH 2), pure water is neutral (pH 7) and soapy water is mildly alkaline.
problem solving

A method for any calculation

  1. Write down what you know, with units.
  2. Write down what you need to find.
  3. Choose the equation that links them.
  4. Rearrange before substituting numbers.
  5. Calculate, then check that the units and size of the answer make sense.
revision

Make your own summary sheets

For each topic, create a single page with the key definitions, one diagram, one worked example and one question you found difficult. Reviewing these short sheets regularly is one of the most effective ways to prepare for tests.

Pair them with our molar mass calculator and balancing practice to check your answers.

chemistry everywhere

Spotting chemistry in daily life

Chemistry is not confined to the laboratory. Rust forming on a bike chain, bread rising, ice melting on a warm day and the fizz of a dissolving tablet are all chemical or physical changes you can explain with the ideas in these guides.

Keeping a short “chemistry spotted” list in your notebook is a fun way to connect lessons to the real world.
